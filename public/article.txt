CHAPTER 1
From Biology to Silicon: The Origin of Synaptic Plasticity
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The brain does not separate learning from operation. Connections between neurons strengthen and weaken as signals pass through them — the very act of processing is the act of learning. This chapter traces that idea from biology to the mathematical abstractions that power modern AI.
1.1  Why Brains Are Different From Programs
A conventional computer program stores data in memory and executes fixed instructions. The instructions do not change as data flows through them. The brain operates differently. Its 'instructions' — the strengths of connections between neurons — change continuously based on recent experience. This entanglement of memory and computation is what we are trying to model.
Understanding how to import this property into artificial systems begins with understanding why biological brains have it in the first place.
1.2  The Neuron: A Signal-Processing Unit
A neuron is a specialized cell that receives electrical signals from thousands of other neurons, integrates them, and — if the total exceeds a threshold — fires its own signal down a long fiber called an axon. The firing event is called an action potential: a brief, stereotyped voltage spike that propagates without diminishing.
The key insight is that neurons do not fire continuously. They fire in discrete pulses, and the timing and rate of those pulses carry information. The brain's computational 'language' is therefore not continuous-valued scalars but spike trains — sequences of discrete events in time.
Architectural Note: The Dragon Hatchling (BDH) architecture explicitly uses a spiking-neuron interpretation of its computational units, which is why understanding the biology is relevant — not just metaphorically but architecturally.
1.3  Synapses: Where Plasticity Lives
Neurons communicate at junctions called synapses. When an action potential arrives at a synapse, it triggers the release of neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft. These molecules bind to receptors on the downstream neuron and either excite or inhibit its tendency to fire.
The critical property is that the strength of this chemical signal — how much influence one neuron exerts on another — is not fixed. It can be modulated by recent activity. This modulation is synaptic plasticity.
Biologically, plasticity is implemented through mechanisms like changes in the number of active receptors, receptor sensitivity, or the amount of neurotransmitter released. For our purposes, the key takeaway is simpler: repeated co-activation of two neurons makes the synapse between them more efficient.
1.4  Plasticity: The Ability to Change
Not all forms of plasticity operate on the same timescale. Short-term synaptic plasticity (milliseconds to minutes) includes:
Facilitation: Repeated stimulation temporarily increases the synaptic response.
Depression: Repeated stimulation temporarily decreases it as neurotransmitter vesicles are depleted.
Long-term plasticity (hours to years) includes Long-Term Potentiation (LTP) and Long-Term Depression (LTD) — the cellular basis of long-term memory. For this article, we focus on short-term facilitation as the biological motivation for temporary associative memory in models.
1.4.1  Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity (STDP)
STDP refines Hebb's idea with timing. The rule is empirical: if neuron A fires before neuron B, the synapse from A to B is strengthened (potentiation). If A fires after B, the synapse is weakened (depression). Strength change decays exponentially with the time difference Δt = t_post − t_pre:
Δw = A₊ · exp(−Δt / τ₊)    if  Δt > 0  (pre before post → potentiation)
Δw = −A₋ · exp( Δt / τ₋)    if  Δt ≤ 0  (post before pre → depression)
Where A₊ and A₋ are learning rate constants, and τ₊, τ₋ are time constants on the order of 20 milliseconds. STDP is not used directly in modern ML models, but it reveals the biological precedent for activity-dependent, local, asymmetric weight updates.
1.5  Hebb's Postulate: 'Fire Together, Wire Together'
In 1949, psychologist Donald Hebb proposed a simple principle to explain learning:
"When an axon of cell A is near enough to excite cell B and repeatedly or persistently takes part in firing it, some growth process or metabolic change takes place in one or both cells such that A's efficiency, as one of the cells firing B, is increased."
— Donald Hebb, The Organization of Behavior, 1949
This is the foundational idea: correlation drives connection strength. Neurons that fire together become more strongly connected. This is not just a biological metaphor — it is a precise mathematical operation that can be implemented in artificial networks.
1.6  The Mathematics of Learning
Let's formalize Hebb's postulate. Suppose we have a pre-synaptic neuron with activity xᵢ and a post-synaptic neuron with activity yⱼ. The weight wᵢⱼ represents the connection strength. Hebb's rule says:
Δwᵢⱼ  =  η · xᵢ · yⱼ
Where η is the learning rate. In matrix form, if we have a pre-synaptic activity vector x ∈ ℝⁿ and post-synaptic vector y ∈ ℝᵐ, the weight update is the outer product:
ΔW  =  η · y xᵀ    ∈  ℝᵐˣⁿ
This outer product is the core operation that will appear throughout this series — in the fast-weight update rule in Chapter 2 and in BDH's synaptic-write mechanism in Chapter 3.
The Problem: Pure Hebbian learning is unstable. Weights grow without bound as active neurons reinforce their own connections.
1.6.1  Oja's Rule: Adding Stability
Finnish mathematician Erkki Oja introduced a stabilizing term in 1982. Instead of unbounded growth, the weight decays proportionally to the squared output:
Δwᵢⱼ  =  η · ( xᵢ yⱼ  −  yⱼ² wᵢⱼ )
The term −yⱼ² wᵢⱼ is a normalization factor. As the weight grows, this decay grows too, eventually preventing runaway amplification. Oja's rule can be shown to converge to the principal component of the input — essentially performing online PCA.
This is the first example of a stability-plasticity trade-off: how do we make a system that can learn new patterns without catastrophically overwriting old ones? This question is central to BDH-CQ in Chapter 3.
1.7  Transition to Silicon
Biological plasticity is a continuous-time process governed by differential equations. Artificial neural networks discretize this into gradient-descent updates computed at the end of a training phase. The result is a hard separation: training time (when weights change) vs. inference time (when weights are frozen).
But the biological premise — that processing and learning can happen simultaneously — suggests another option: what if some weights remain temporarily mutable during inference?
1.7.1  Fast Weights vs. Slow Weights
Geoffrey Hinton coined the distinction: slow weights are the standard trained parameters, updated only during backpropagation. Fast weights are a secondary, temporary weight matrix that can be written to and read from during a single inference pass.
The slow weights encode general learned behavior. The fast weights encode recent context — effectively a form of working memory. A single network can therefore have two kinds of knowledge simultaneously: persistent skills (slow) and rapidly acquired associations (fast).
hₜ  =  f( W_slow · xₜ  +  W_fast,t · xₜ )
Where W_fast,t is updated each step via the outer product rule. This is the bridge from biological synaptic plasticity to the computational mechanism we implement in Chapter 2.

CHAPTER 2
Associative Memory & Fast Weights in Models
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Standard neural networks freeze all their parameters the moment training ends. This chapter examines what that costs — and then shows how outer-product associative memory provides an alternative working memory with a bounded footprint.
2.1  The Problem of Working Memory
A language model that can't update its internal state during inference can only 'remember' by keeping earlier tokens visible in its context window. This works, but it has a cost: the larger the context, the more memory and compute the model needs at every generation step.
What if some of that recent context could live inside the network's weights — temporarily, in a bounded store — rather than in an ever-growing external buffer?
2.2  The Fixed-Weight Paradigm
In a standard feedforward or Transformer model, there is a clean separation:
Training phase: Weights θ are updated via backpropagation and gradient descent to minimize a loss function over a dataset.
Inference phase: Weights θ are completely frozen. The model is a fixed function f_θ(x).
This is clean and fast. But it means the network's 'knowledge' is permanently compiled into its weights — any new association (e.g., something that happened 3 tokens ago) must either be re-derived from context tokens at every step or stored externally.
2.3  Attention and the KV Cache
Recall how self-attention works. For each token at position t, we compute three projections from its embedding xₜ:
qₜ = W_Q · xₜ,    kₜ = W_K · xₜ,    vₜ = W_V · xₜ
To generate token t, the model attends over all previous positions 1 … t−1:
Attn(qₜ, K, V)  =  softmax( qₜ Kᵀ / √dₖ ) · V
To avoid recomputing kₛ and vₛ for every earlier position s at each new step, a KV cache stores them. As generation proceeds, the cache grows.
2.3.1  KV Cache Memory Footprint
Let L = number of layers, H = number of attention heads, dₖ = head dimension, and T = context length. The KV cache stores both key and value tensors for every layer, head, and token:
KV Cache size  =  2 × L × H × dₖ × T × (bytes per element)
With T growing linearly with context length, memory scales as O(T). For a 70B model with 8192-token context in fp16, the KV cache can consume over 80 GB — larger than the model weights themselves.
2.3.2  The Memory Bandwidth Bottleneck
At each decode step, the GPU must read the entire KV cache from memory to compute attention. Arithmetic operations are fast; moving data from HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) is the bottleneck. Inference throughput is limited not by compute but by memory bandwidth.
This is the fundamental motivation for alternatives that maintain a fixed-size state rather than a growing cache.
2.4  Associative Memory: A Fixed-Size Alternative
The key insight from Chapter 1 carries over: the outer product y xᵀ writes an association between a key vector x and a value vector y into a weight matrix. We can use this to build a memory that stores recent context without growing.
2.4.1  The Outer-Product Update Rule
Define a memory matrix Mₜ ∈ ℝᵈˣᵈ. At each time step, we update it using the current key-value pair:
Mₜ  =  λ · Mₜ₋₁       (decay old memory)
    +  η · ( vₜ ⊗ kₜ )   (write new association)
Where:
λ ∈ (0, 1] — retention factor (how much of old memory is preserved)
η — plasticity factor (how strongly the new association is written)
vₜ ⊗ kₜ = vₜ kₜᵀ — the outer product associating key kₜ with value vₜ
The memory matrix size is fixed at O(d²) regardless of context length. This is the space-complexity advantage over the KV cache.
2.4.2  Retrieval and Inference
To retrieve the value associated with a query q, we apply the memory matrix:
v̂  =  Mₜ · q
If the memory contains an association M = v kᵀ, then:
v̂  =  v kᵀ q  =  v · ⟨k, q⟩
The retrieved value is the stored value v scaled by the similarity ⟨k, q⟩ between the stored key and the query. This is linear attention — a form of attention without the softmax normalization, which allows the memory to remain a compact matrix state instead of an explicit token list.
2.5  The Fundamental Trade-Off
Fixed-size associative memory has a capacity limit. When multiple associations are superimposed into the same matrix, they can interfere. If two different queries q₁, q₂ are mapped to the same region of key space, retrieval becomes noisy:
M q₁  =  (v₁k₁ᵀ + v₂k₂ᵀ) q₁
      =  v₁⟨k₁,q₁⟩  +  v₂⟨k₂,q₁⟩
The second term is interference. When keys are not orthogonal, retrieved values are corrupted.

CHAPTER 3
Brain Dragon Hatchling: BDH & BDH-CQ
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Brain Dragon Hatchling (BDH) is a published sequence architecture that takes the biological analogy seriously as an architectural design choice — not just a metaphor. This chapter unpacks its graph-based structure, the equations governing its synaptic writes, and how BDH-CQ extends this to real-time skill acquisition from demonstrations.
3.1  What BDH Is (and Is Not)
BDH is not a biological simulation. It is a GPU-efficient sequence model that uses a brain-inspired representation as its computational backbone. The authors describe a network of locally interacting 'neuron particles' whose connections — synaptic weights — can change during inference using Hebbian-style updates.
This is architecturally significant: BDH does not use the Transformer's global attention mechanism. Instead of every token attending to every other token, computation is local — each particle interacts with a neighborhood, not the entire graph. This changes the scaling behavior fundamentally.
Evidence Boundary: Evidence note (Kosowski et al., 2025): The authors report language modeling, translation, and interpretability results. The architectural claims in this chapter derive from the published paper.
3.2  Beyond the Transformer Stack
A standard Transformer is a regular graph: every token in a layer is connected to every other token via attention. The graph is dense, uniform, and grows quadratically with sequence length.
BDH replaces this with a scale-free network. In a scale-free network (following a power-law degree distribution):
Most nodes (neurons) have few connections.
A small number of hub neurons have many connections.
Local neighborhoods exist — neurons primarily influence those nearby.
This mirrors the structure observed in cortical connectivity maps. It also enables sparse, local computation — a major efficiency advantage over dense attention.
3.2.1  Scale-Free Neuron Graph
Let G = (V, E) be the neuron graph. The degree distribution follows a power law:
P(deg(v) = k)  ∝  k^(−α),    α > 1
Activity at each node is sparse and positive. Only a small fraction of neurons are active at any given time step. This sparsity is enforced — it is not an emergent property. It ensures that synaptic writes are selective and that interference between associations is minimized.
3.2.2  Spiking Representation
BDH interprets its neuron activations as spike rates. A neuron's 'activity' sᵢᵗ ≥ 0 represents how frequently it fires at time step t. The sparse constraint means most sᵢᵗ = 0.
This connects to the biology from Chapter 1: the model's computational units are genuine (discrete-time) analogs of spiking neurons, not continuous sigmoidal activations. Working memory is then carried not by which neurons are active, but by the synaptic weights connecting them — exactly the biological mechanism for short-term plasticity.
3.3  BDH: Core Equations
Let Wᵗ ∈ ℝⁿˣⁿ be the synaptic weight matrix at time step t, where n is the number of neurons. sᵗ ∈ ℝⁿ≥₀ is the sparse activity vector.
3.3.1  Synaptic Write Mechanism
At each time step, new experience writes into the synaptic state via a Hebbian outer product:
Wᵗ  =  λ · Wᵗ⁻¹  +  η · sᵗ ⊗ sᵗ
Or expanded to the pre-post asymmetric form (analogous to STDP from Chapter 1):
Wᵢⱼᵗ  =  λ · Wᵢⱼᵗ⁻¹  +  η · sᵢᵗ · sⱼᵗ⁻¹
Where:
Wᵢⱼᵗ — synaptic weight from neuron j to neuron i at time t
sᵢᵗ — post-synaptic activity (neuron i at time t)
sⱼᵗ⁻¹ — pre-synaptic activity (neuron j at the previous step)
λ — synaptic decay / forgetting factor
η — plasticity rate
3.3.2  Local Hebbian Updates
Because the graph is sparse, this update only needs to be computed for edges (i, j) ∈ E. This makes the complexity proportional to the number of edges, not n²:
ΔWᵢⱼ  =  η · sᵢᵗ · sⱼᵗ⁻¹    for all  (i,j) ∈ E
The next-step activity of neuron i is computed from its local neighborhood:
sᵢᵗ⁺¹  =  σ(  Σⱼ∈N(i)  Wᵢⱼᵗ · sⱼᵗ  +  bᵢ  )
Where N(i) is the local neighborhood of neuron i, and σ enforces non-negativity and sparsity (e.g., a ReLU with top-k selection).
3.4  BDH-CQ: Continuous-Query Skill Acquisition
BDH-CQ (Continuous Query) extends BDH to the problem of learning from demonstrations. The setting: an agent observes a cue-action pair and must retrieve the correct action when given only the cue again — without updating any slow (backprop-trained) weights.
This is the machine learning analogue of the biological scenario from Chapter 1: a single experience temporarily potentiates a pathway, changing the circuit's behavior for subsequent stimuli.
3.4.1  Learning from a Demonstration
Suppose we observe a demonstration: cue c should be followed by action a. In BDH-CQ terms, the system encodes this as:
k  =  φ(c),    v  =  ψ(a)
Where φ, ψ are fixed projection functions (encoded in the slow weights). The association is written into the recurrent synaptic state:
Wᵗ  =  λ · Wᵗ⁻¹  +  η · v ⊗ k
No backpropagation occurs. No slow weight is changed. The association lives entirely in the fast, temporary synaptic state.
3.4.2  Recurrent State Update Rule
BDH-CQ maintains a continuously updated hidden state hₜ alongside the synaptic matrix Wₜ. The recurrent update combines both:
hₜ₊₁  =  f( W_slow · xₜ  +  Wᵗ · hₜ )
The slow weights W_slow provide general language understanding. The fast weights Wᵗ carry the demonstration-acquired association. At query time, if the cue is presented again:
v̂  =  Wᵗ · φ(c')
  =  (λWᵗ⁻¹ + η v kᵀ) · φ(c')
If c' ≈ c (the query cue matches the demonstration cue), then ⟨k, φ(c')⟩ ≈ ‖k‖² is large, and the retrieved value approximates v:
v̂  ≈  η · ‖k‖² · v  +  residual from Wᵗ⁻¹
3.4.3  The Full BDH-CQ Inference Loop
At each token position t, the full BDH-CQ inference step is:
Step 1 — Activation:
  sᵗ  =  TopK( ReLU( Σⱼ∈N(i) Wᵢⱼᵗ⁻¹ sⱼᵗ⁻¹  +  W_slow · xₜ ) )
Step 2 — Plasticity (Synaptic Write):
  Wᵗ  =  λ · Wᵗ⁻¹  +  η · sᵗ ⊗ sᵗ⁻¹
Step 3 — Decode:
  yₜ  =  Decode( sᵗ )
Three coupled operations per step:
Activation: Compute sparse spiking activities from local neighborhood and slow-weight input.
Plasticity: Write the new co-activation pattern into the synaptic matrix.
Decode: Read out the next token prediction from the current activities.
Crucially, steps 1 and 2 are coupled — the activity that produces the output also updates the weights. Learning and inference are inseparable, just as in biological neural circuits.
