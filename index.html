Learning in the moment

A neural network usually learns during training. But what if a new example could leave a temporary trace—strong enough to guide the very next decision?

Imagine being shown a blue circle and told to move left. You do not need months of practice; for a short while, the cue and the action are linked in memory. Some machine-learning systems use an analogous idea: they preserve a rapidly changing state alongside their slowly trained parameters.

This article builds that intuition with one deliberately small model. It does not reproduce a brain or a complete research architecture. It lets us inspect one claim precisely: compatible demonstrations strengthen a temporary association, while time and competing demonstrations can weaken its recall.

The diagram below begins with an empty memory. Press Add demonstration once. The blue-circle cue writes strength into the “move left” route. Press it again and the same route becomes easier to recall.

The lines are not decorative. Their brightness and width are calculated from the two association values shown underneath. Testing simply selects the stronger route; when neither route is stronger, the honest answer is “uncertain.”

Second experiment

When a new example contradicts the old one

Now suppose someone uses the same blue circle to demonstrate “move right.” The new example competes for the same cue. Before pressing the button, make a prediction: will the original route remain stronger?

Retention between examples0.86

Retention controls how much of the previous temporary state survives before a compatible example is added. A value of 1 keeps all of it; 0.5 keeps half.

Under the surface

The rule, one piece at a time

There is no mysterious equation behind the interaction. Each compatible demonstration performs two understandable operations:

1

Keep part of the old memory.
old strength × retention

2

Add the new evidence.
retained strength + 0.32

3

Stop at full strength.
the result cannot exceed 1

new strength=old strength × retention+new evidence

A conflicting example reduces the original route to 72% of its current value and adds 0.42 to the competing route. These constants are teaching choices, not fitted biological measurements. They make strengthening and interference visible without pretending to be a complete neural model.

Research connection

What the papers support—and what they do not

Fast-weight and synaptic-plasticity research motivates the separation between slow trained parameters and quickly changing state. The Dragon Hatchling studies inference-time working memory through synaptic plasticity; related work connects fast weights with linear attention and rapid adaptation.
